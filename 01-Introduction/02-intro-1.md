## Amaroo, the System

Amaroo is a *method and environment* for the creation, maintenance, and sustenance of blockchains. It is a principled blockchain factory, and a place for blockchains to live in a free, open, and thriving super-network.

Typical Amaroo blockchains (the vast majority of which are *dapp chains*, or just *chains*) each run exactly one dapp. A dapp (in Amaroo) is a collection of modules. Those modules each provide some specific functionality. There are some core modules that each dapp chain needs for it to be part of Amaroo, but there are no restrictions beyond those few requirements: modules (or a combination of them) can do anything deterministic that *any* blockchain can do. Amaroo itself has some core modules, too. namely: modules for specifying chains, deploying new chains, and ensuring consistency and interoperability between chains.

Amaroo chains are immutable -- they cannot be upgraded once they are deployed. This is atypical. But, why should we host an immutable transaction ledger in a database with mutable rules? The normal justifications are things like: adding new features, changing chain parameters, fixing mistakes, and preventing attacks. None of these *intrinsically* require mutable chains, though. Adding features can be done by deploying new dapp chains (which is relatively fast and easy, similar to deploying new backwards-compatible smart contracts on Ethereum); changing chain parameters requires a hard-fork when those parameters are embedded at the wrong level of emergence (i.e. deep in the blockchain's protocol, rather than closer to dapp logic), but, with better organization and structure, changing parameters can be done simply via deploying a new dapp chain; fixing mistakes and error correction is more complex and nuanced, so will be discussed later, though builds on techniques of good-structure and publishing new versions; and preventing attacks can be done via a combination of the previous methods provided that care is taken to avoid coding yourself in to a corner. All of this is to say: hard forks are not necessary when working within well-structured protocols that treat chain parameters as dapp logic rather than fundamentals of the network. There are deeper and better principles that we can use for distributed networks than have been used in the past.

Immutable chains mean that users will never be coerced to use dapps and protocols they do not wish to use. Non-coercion is an important property that is deeply related to the values upon which blockchains and distributed protocols are built. One reason these values are important is because they create an environment of high standards and rational interactions. How can two people agree on something? Within the scope of the topic, they must be either irrational in the same way (and there are many ways to be irrational), or rational (and there is only one way to be rational) -- how else could they reach legitimate agreement? If we allow coercion, even the sort of mass-coercion that democracies sometimes commit against their own people, then it is an acknowledgement of a failure *either* in standards *or* rationality. However, if we hold ourselves to standards of non-coercion, then the failure of a majority or of elites is *their own failure* and it *harms only them* (for the most part). Coercion is allowed in networks like Bitcoin, Ethereum, and other blockchains and DLTs -- evidenced by the creation of new networks out of semi-failed upgrades. If coercion is allowed then those failures are socialized across the user-base. The users that stood against the change (which was eventually forced upon them) might ask the question: "what could I have done differently to avoid this?" to which the only answer is: "you could have never participated, or you could have left." That is an answer which leads to dead or dystopic networks. I am interested in networks that *flourish*, and thus a core principle of Amaroo is *non-coercion*. To be free, you need assurance that you will remain free.

For users to freely interact with Amaroo dapp chains they must have some assurance of non-censorship; it is for this reason that, technically, Amaroo chains can be DAGs rather than trees/chains. There are some advantages to this, such as a more democratized block production (i.e. mining) protocol. The DAG structure allows for blocks to be produced more readily and in a highly distributed fashion; for miners it will be more like participating in a mining pool than a centralizing network like Bitcoin or Ethereum. This is due to a block-DAG's ability to merge histories and more flexible requirements around the proof required for a block to be valid. The DAG structure (named Quanta -- nb: draft name from 2014) is a super-set of the GHOST protocol. This means Amaroo DAGs - in essence - inherit some of the simplicity and reliability compared to more radical data structures like IOTA's Tangle. Furthermore, Quanta DAGs are able to be reasoned about as blockchains, which simplifies thinking about things like consensus, cross-chain communications, SPV, etc. Amaroo chains don't need to be DAGs, their foundational data structure can be trees/chains too. It might seem like this introduces extreme overhead for matters like the total size of a blockchain, but we should still expect that the total size of all transactions over a given period to dwarf the size of the associated block headers. We can also design Amaroo consensus systems to balance the ratio of block-header-bytes to transaction-bytes so that SPV systems are not bottlenecked by the volume of block-headers. There are additional and preexisting ways of reducing this overhead too, such as special 'epoch' blocks that 'summarize' the events since the last epoch block. The main point is that DAG based chains can still leave enough excess capacity in dependant systems so that those systems are not constrained by the volume of block headers. The impact of using DAGs is further reduced via the emergent and efficient distribution of mining and node resources over the Amaroo ecosystem.

note: the quanta DAG system is not related to https://www.quanta.im/

Amaroo is designed so that no single user needs to run all the Amaroo blockchains; they can just run the ones they're interested in. This means that the full Amaroo network can be larger than the resources available to any single user; something that is a major constraint for some blockchains, e.g. Bitcoin. Instead of the traditional model, where a user running a 'full node' would verify all transactions on the network, 'full nodes' on the Amaroo network verify all transactions only for individual *dapps*. In this way, Amaroo is a super-network of dapp-networks; dapp-networks being the networks facilitating the respective dapp chains. Even though each dapp-network may be constrained by the maximum resources of a single computer (though parallelization methods do exist, so we can overcome this constraint), the super-network is constrained in proportion to the sum of all computers. That figure is so vast that we can conclude there is excess capacity (and thus it is not a bottleneck) with very little effort. This 'sharding' methodology (each dapp has its own shard) means that resources are distributed across Amaroo roughly according to activity and utility. Another way to say this is: congestion in Amaroo affects only those people doing the congesting.

One advantage of Amaroo's resource distribution qualities is that fees are not the only method to prevent spam: dapp design can prevent spam too; thus, sometimes, *fees are not necessary*. One of the reasons that spam transactions have traditionally been a problem for other blockchains (like Bitcoin, Ethereum, etc) is that those networks are designed to be highly general systems that are capable of many things at once; they're designed to service many user needs on one chain. Amaroo chains are the opposite of this: they're specific to one and only one dapp. Spam transactions aren't necessarily an issue for dapps because dapps can be designed so that *any* valid transaction is a useful and meaningful transaction. In this way some Amaroo chains can operate without fees, provided a suitable design can be created. Some dapps (such as a dapp to run EVM code; i.e. emulate Ethereum) cannot realise a fee-free quality because their purpose is to provide systems that are general purpose, and thus a fee-free policy would lead to a tragedy of the commons. However, by restricting the operations of a dapp (e.g. a decentralized exchange), it is possible to allow only those operations that are mutually beneficial to all participants. It's relatively easy to see how this can work for an exchange: everyone benefits with more liquidity and volume; why would market participants prefer a market that discourages participation? That doesn't make sense, except in highly engineered hypotheticals which are unlikely to occur in reality.

There is, however, a deeper reason to desire this kind of flexible, utilization driven resource distribution; one that has reach beyond blockchains and distributed networks: resilience. In Goldratt's *It's Not Luck* (1994) he conjectures that companies can maintain indefinite competitiveness, profitability, and market relevance by structuring their resources in a particular way. That is: flexibility should be created by ensuring that each employee serves multiple market segments (some of which might be new segments created by virtue of the company's offerings) and a company should not segment their resources to match market segments. In each market segment the company should maintain a dominant competitive edge via unconventional thinking (the main topic of the book), but the company should not take the entire market segment -- they should leave some excess capacity in the market segment. The company should also choose (or create) segments that are not tightly coupled, so that a drop in one segment typically does not coincide with a drop in a different segment. These three things allow a company to exploit lucrative opportunities when they arise (because their resources are flexible), shift resources away from segments for which demand drops (because they operate in multiple uncoupled segments), and meet the three key goals of a company: serve the employees, serve the shareholders, and serve the market. Sadly it is still a mainstream idea that these three goals are in conflict, and -- with bad strategy -- they remain in conflict. It should not be surprising that, when silver bullets are found, there is a notable and significant convergence between the new ideas and other, battle-tested and enduring ideas from completely different areas. Consider how resilient ecosystems differ from fragile ecosystems. Are those differences not deeply related to the differences between resilient and fragile companies, or even countries? Are those differences not also apparent between resilient and fragile cultures, networks, and even individuals? Why should it be surprising that we find similar differences between resilient and fragile blockchains? No, it is not surprising that we should find such differences; ones that converge with deep truths. What *is* surprising are the ideas and methods of construction to achieve them. To date, those ideas have not been brought together, and *that* is what follows.
