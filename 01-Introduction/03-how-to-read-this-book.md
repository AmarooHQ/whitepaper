## How to Read This Book

Most books will not tell you how you should read them. That's fine for most fiction and simple stuff, but if we're dealing with many new ideas -- especially when some of those ideas are foundational for other ideas -- then the typical methods of reading will fail the reader. Books about complex ideas fail when the author either doesn't know their audience or doesn't tell a wide audience how to read the book.

You should read this book slowly, carefully, and stop if you get stuck (e.g., you disagree with some contents). If you proceed when you get stuck, then you'll make compounding errors that jeopardize the success of your project that is 'read TAW'. (nb: TAW means The Amaroo Whitepaper.) Instead, you should focus, reread, and attempt to understand the section that troubles you. Either one of two things are true: your disagreement is rooted in a valid, decisive criticism -- in which case you will have my gratitude for telling me about it -- or, more likely, you have other ideas that are in conflict, and those other ideas are the problem. (A third option might be possible too: you have thought of a decisive criticism; however, if that's the case then I have not done my job: to vet my work.)

It is naive to think that you won't have troubles reading this book. For one, most of you will have some static memes about environmentalism which will be challenged -- those memes are the same ones that have lead to the over-emphasis on proof-of-stake consensus mechanisms. A common 'red flag' for this idea is the description of proof-of-work as 'wasteful'. That is a misconception that most blockchain academics have not realized, though that is not unexpected because they are not philosophers (and thus their ability to do high quality thinking is limited). If this paragraph *offends* you, then you have serious work to do improving your thinking methods, and I suggest you put this book down and look to easier, foundational philosophical works (good places to start are the essays on fallibleideas.com, Goldratt's *The Choice*, and Rand's *Philosophy: Who Needs It*). Eventually you should move up to Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity* (BoI) after you have *mastered* more basic reading and thinking methods. The advice in this section applies to reading those sort of works too, and especially BoI.

If you have the humility to accept (at least the possibility of) the above paragraph, then it may be safe to proceed. Just go slow, take it easy, that sort of thing. Take notes. Discuss things you don't understand. Build up a mastery of the foundations before you try to tackle complex ideas. If you do that, then you can succeed at anything.

This book is written so that you do not need to backtrack; though you will need to do that if you don't read slowly and deliberately. Every sentence has been written so that it is clear and precise. There has also been considerable effort to keep sentences (and particularly clauses) simple. It will not read like an academic work because most academics do not value those things.

Good luck.
