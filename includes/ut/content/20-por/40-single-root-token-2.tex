
The first example context is \emph{one network split over two chains}.
Both chains use the same \emph{root token}.
There are, naturally, questions to answer,
    like \emph{How are block rewards managed?}
    and \emph{How can the root token be on two blockchains at once?}

Let's consider the following \emph{restricted case} first: the chains (L and R) both have a two-way peg between, ensuring that no coins are ever created or destroyed.

In this case, \autoref{eq:por-conv-work} collapses to
\begin{align}
    \text{ConvWork}_{R\rightarrow L}(w) = \; & \frac{L_d}{L_r} \cdot \frac{R_r}{R_d} \cdot w % \cdot \cancelto{1}{X_{R\rightarrow L}}
        & & % \text{R-hashes }\rightarrow\text{ L-hashes}
        & & \text{Since }X_{R\rightarrow L}=1
        \nonumber
        \\[0.5em]
    \implies \text{ConvWork}_{R\rightarrow L}(R_d) = \; & \frac{L_d}{L_r} \cdot R_r
        & & \text{L-hashes}
        & & \text{R-blocks weigh } w = R_d \cdot 1 \text{ R-hashes}
        \nonumber
        \\[0.5em]
    \implies \text{ConvWork}_{R\rightarrow L}(R_d) \cdot \frac{L_r}{L_d} = \; & R_r
        & & \text{L-coins}
        & & \text{Weight of an R-block in L-coins}
        \label{eq:por-conv-work-srt}
    % \therefore \text{ConvReward}_{R\rightarrow L}(w) = \; & \frac{R_r}{R_d} \cdot w
    %     & & \text{R-hashes -> L-coins}
    %     & & \text{The weight of an } \text{R-block in L-coins}
    %     \label{eq:por-conv-work-srt}
\end{align}

If we convert like this (treating 1 R-block as worth $R_r \cdot 1$ L-coins) then we'll we need to convert the result to L-hashes \emph{eventually} -- but we can sum this up if we had multiple reflections to process (since $\nicefrac{L_r}{L_d}$ is a constant for some given moment in time).
It's worth remembering that block-weight is measured \emph{per block}.
Since we're only converting a single block's weight at a time, the \emph{per block} part of the resulting units \emph{cancels out}.

But where do we get the reflecting chain's block reward?
To start with, we can't let each chain \emph{set} its own reward, that would break the two-way peg.
It also means \emph{the reflecting chain shouldn't be the source of that data}.
We could retrieve it from state (if it's already calculated), but for the sake of this example, let's calculate it in the \textsc{WeightOf} function.

Right now, we don't have enough \emph{context} to know what to do; \emph{what contexts would be useful?}

What \emph{purpose} does a block reward fulfil?
It must be something about \emph{security}, because that's how miners are motivated to mine.
If we have multiple chains (with the same root token), why should one chain be more secure than another?
One reason is because \emph{more commerce happens there;} i.e., more of the network relies on \emph{that} chain compared to the other one.

What would make sense, given the context of a commerce-imbalance, to base the block reward on?
A straightforward answer (which can be globally known, too) is \emph{the ratio of root tokens on each chain.}
We can have a \emph{network wide} rule that there is some \emph{network wide inflation rate}, and each chain's block reward is the product of the \emph{ratio} of coins on that chain with the entire supply.
This way, the global inflation rate is predictable, even though the coins produced \emph{on each chain} (via block rewards) are variable.
Moreover, we should expect that the \emph{value to the community} of each chain roughly matches the \emph{distribution of the community's activities}, and the \emph{distribution of security}, too.
This method is also consistent with the idea of \emph{equal work for equal reward}.
We can rely on that idea because \emph{equality of work} is a result of the market (for block rewards) formed by participating miners.

If the network uses this rule to determine block reward, then, for either chain $C$, what can we say?
Let: $I$ be the network-wide inflation rate (coins/s); $G_t$ be the network-wide root token supply (coins); and $C_t$ be the number of root tokens on chain $C$ (coins). $C_f$ is the frequency of chain $C$ (blocks/s).

\begin{align}
    C_r \cdot C_f & = \frac{C_t}{G_t} \cdot I
        & & \frac{\text{C-coins}}{\text{second}}
        & & \text{As set above}
        \nonumber
        \\[0.5em]
    \implies C_r & = \frac{C_t \cdot I}{G_t \cdot C_f}
        & & \frac{\text{C-coins}}{\text{C-block}}
        & & \label{eq:srt-reward}
        \\[0.5em]
    \therefore \frac{R_r}{L_r} =& \; \frac{R_t \cdot I}{G_t \cdot R_f} \cdot \frac{G_t \cdot L_f}{L_t \cdot I}
        & & \frac{\text{L-blocks}}{\text{R-block}}
        & & \text{Via \autoref{eq:srt-reward}}
        \nonumber
        \\[0.0em]
    \frac{R_r}{L_r} =& \; \frac{R_t \cdot L_f}{L_t \cdot R_f} \cdot \cancelto{1}{\frac{G_t \cdot I}{G_t \cdot I}}
        & & \frac{\text{L-blocks}}{\text{R-block}}
        & & \text{by \emph{definition}}
        \label{eq:srt-block-ratios}
\end{align}

Hold up!
Didn't we just go over -- in great detail -- why we can't use block frequencies ($L_f$ and $R_f$) in conversions?
Why is it okay to use them now?

To answer this, let's consider \emph{how} block rewards could be consistently set network-wide.
Unlike a chain's difficulty, the block reward needs to be consistent with some global inflation rate.
\autoref{eq:srt-block-ratios} implies that $R_r \cdot R_f \cdot L_t = L_r \cdot L_f \cdot R_t$, so in some cases \emph{we don't need to know the global state, just that this relationship is enforced for local state.}
That said, if $I$ is known across the network (i.e., it is constant or a well known function) then calculating $G_t$ is trivial: $G_t = I * l + G_{t_0}$, where $l$ is the lifetime of the network (in seconds), and $G_{t_0}$ is any initial root token supply that was not created via inflation (e.g., via some token generation event).

Could we maintain consistency across chains via something like a \textbf{Reward Adjustment Algorithm} (RAA)?
If each chain commits to the input values (e.g., via the inclusion of $C_t$ in their block-headers), then an RAA could be evaluated using only the header-chain (similar to the DAA).
This way, if a miner (or a chain) tries to subvert or attack the RAA, it's trivially detectable by reflecting chains.

If we introduced something like an RAA, then the RAA \emph{output} could become \emph{context laden} (depending on the RAA's input parameters).
In the case of a global inflation rate, \emph{this means that chains would rely on a common meaning of ``seconds''!}
So it is by \emph{construction and definition} that the block frequencies introduced in \autoref{eq:srt-reward} becomes part of the convertible context.
Thus, we can \emph{now} include them when we couldn't before.

Note that we also \emph{prove} \autoref{eq:lfrf-ne-rrlr} for this case.
We know that -- when $L_f$ and $R_f$ are in the convertible context -- there is a \emph{scaling factor} that typically $\ne 1$.
The reason we can find a scaling factor \emph{here} (but not generally) is that we \emph{included it in a protocol-level relationship} (\autoref{eq:srt-reward}).
